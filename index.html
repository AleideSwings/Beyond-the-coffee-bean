Central Thesis & Analytical Stance

Beyond the Tech Utopia: Why Digital Traceability Is Not Automatically Fair

In policy briefings and corporate ESG announcements, the European Union Deforestation Regulation (EUDR, Regulation 2023/1115) is celebrated as an objective, satellite-driven triumph. Geolocation coordinates (latitude and longitude) coupled with orbital imagery promise to cleanly excise deforestation from European supply chains.

However, as empirical research in the Central Highlands (Dak Lak, Lam Dong, Gia Lai, Dak Nong) confirms, digital mapping and NGO interventions do not inherently produce fairness. Instead, data collection mechanisms interact with deep-seated structural inequalities: fragmented land plots, ethnic minority language barriers, multi-tiered trader intermediaries, and ambiguous state forestry boundaries. Traceability can easily become an instrument of risk offloading, where downstream European roasters demand total legal certainty while leaving smallholders to shoulder the administrative and financial weight.

Conceptual Framework

The Three Dimensions of Smallholder Fairness

To evaluate whether compliance systems genuinely protect producers or merely police them, we analyze the EUDR transition across three distinct dimensions of fairness:

Dimension 1

Equitable Distribution of Compliance Costs

Economic Justice

While multinational roasters and retail chains command high margins on consumer coffee, farmgate bean prices fluctuate dramatically. Meeting EUDR standards requires digital polygon surveys, GPS validation, deforestation screening, and legal audits. Surveys from the Sustainable Agricultural Supply Chains Initiative (SASI) and Wageningen University show that less than 5% of smallholders receive direct technical or financial subsidies from EU buyers.

Instead, the costs of GPS boundary verification (averaging $35 to $60 per farm plot) are quietly pushed down to local cooperatives or deducted from collector purchase prices. For a family cultivating a 0.8-hectare farm with net annual earnings under $2,000, compliance expenses can swallow 3% to 8% of annual household income—whereas for a European trading conglomerate, the per-kilogram software cost is negligible (fractions of a cent).

Dimension 2

Market Access & The Risk of 'Safe-Zone' Exclusion

Inclusion & Leakage

The EUDR imposes severe sanctions for non-compliance, including fines up to 4% of EU turnover and confiscation of shipments. Under intense regulatory scrutiny, international coffee buyers naturally gravitate towards simplicity and certainty. In Brazil, an importer can survey a single 500-hectare automated plantation with one polygon. In Vietnam's Central Highlands, sourcing that same 500 hectares requires mapping up to 550 separate smallholder families, checking 550 title deeds, and reconciling multiple contested cadastral borders.

This creates a perverse incentive known as 'Safe-Zone Cherry-Picking': buyers withdraw from complex forest-edge regions and vulnerable ethnic minority communities, opting instead for large, well-documented farms. As observed by researchers (Forest Trees and Livelihoods, 2025; Wageningen, 2023), excluded farmers are forced to sell at steep discounts to non-EU markets (such as China or local instant coffee processors) or switch land use to less regulated crops. Crucially, excluding smallholders does not stop deforestation; it merely drives leakage while destroying rural livelihoods.

"If buyers simply avoid sourcing from forest-adjacent communities to minimize paperwork, the poorest farmers are pushed out of the supply chain—ironically increasing poverty-driven pressure on remaining natural forests."
Ethical Trade Denmark & IDH Landscape Review
Dimension 3

Data Sovereignty, Privacy & Data Extraction

Digital Rights

To satisfy European customs declarations, smallholders are asked to disclose the exact GPS coordinates of their farms, crop yield volumes, and personal identification to commercial apps managed by multinational exporters and agtech vendors. Yet rarely are farmers informed about who stores this geospatial data, how long it is retained, or whether it will be used for carbon credit monetization, predictive commodity pricing, or commercial audits.

Furthermore, in rural Vietnam, uploading coordinates of farms cultivated on historically ambiguous forestry borders exposes farmers to potential tenure disputes or administrative fines by local forestry inspectors. True fairness demands data sovereignty: smallholders and local farmer cooperatives must retain ownership of their agricultural polygons, with clear consent protocols and guaranteed compensation for sharing proprietary farm intelligence.

Key Insight from SASI / GIZ Learning Report (2024):

Open-source, farmer-centric platforms (like those piloted by Fairfood and Tradin Organic) demonstrate that unless farmers receive explicit data incentives and retain cooperative data governance, digital systems become purely extractive, leading to farmer distrust, incorrect coordinates, and eventual data abandonment.

Regulatory Analysis

Formal EUDR Mandate vs. Downstream Buyer 'Gold-Plating'

A crucial finding in supply chain governance is the gap between what Regulation (EU) 2023/1115 actually requires by law and what risk-averse European roasters and multinational exporters demand in practice. To insulate themselves from regulatory liability, buyers routinely "gold-plate" compliance, shifting disproportionate burdens onto Vietnamese producers:

Compliance Area EUDR Legal Obligation (EU Law) Buyer & Exporter Demand ('Gold-Plating')
Plot Geolocation Article 9: Plots under 4 hectares require only a single GPS point (latitude & longitude to 6 decimal places). Polygons are only legally mandated for plots ≥4 ha. Exporters and EU brands demand full multi-point polygon boundaries for every 0.4 ha garden, transferring surveying labor and mobile app licensing fees to local farmers.
Land Tenure & Legality Article 3: Commodities must be produced in accordance with relevant national legislation regarding land use, labor, and environmental rights. Demanding formal updated "Red Books" (LURCs) on 100% of plots, immediately disqualifying farmers with customary tenure, family inheritance transfers, or pending commune reviews.
Deforestation Cut-off Article 2: Coffee must not be produced on land deforested or degraded after 31 December 2020. Deforestation prior to 2020 is not prohibited by EUDR. Refusing to buy from any farm located within 1–2 km of a national park or protected area ("buffer zone blacklisting"), regardless of whether the farm was established 30 years ago.
Liability & Risk Transfer Article 4: EU operators placing products on the Union market retain ultimate legal responsibility for the Due Diligence Statement (DDS). Inserting punitive indemnity clauses in local supplier contracts, demanding that Vietnamese exporters and cooperatives reimburse European buyers for any EU border fines or rejected shipments.
Private Certifications Recital 52: Voluntary certification systems (Fairtrade, Rainforest Alliance, 4C) can support risk assessment, but are not mandatory and cannot substitute for due diligence. Requiring farmers to maintain existing costly private certifications on top of submitting raw EUDR GPS coordinates, multiplying administrative audit fatigue.

The Satellite Blindspot: 'Rolling Up the Sleeve' & Remote Sensing Limits

In international discussions, deforestation is often imagined as catastrophic industrial clearing—bulldozers razing hundreds of hectares of pristine jungle. In Vietnam's coffee heartland, however, encroachment takes a profoundly different form.

The 'Cuốn Chiếu' (Rolling Up the Sleeve) Strategy

As revealed by senior GIS consultant Nguyen Thi Thuc Anh, who advises NGOs in Son La and the Central Highlands, recent surges in global coffee prices have renewed incentives for subtle farm expansion. Rather than clearing visible swathes, farmers apply a gradual technique known locally as cuốn chiếu ("rolling up the mat or sleeve").

A farmer quietly clears a narrow strip of natural forest undergrowth—frequently only 40 to 50 centimeters wide—just enough to plant an extra row of Arabica or Robusta bushes along the forest margin. A year later, another 50 centimeters is claimed.

The Resolution Trap

Standard optical satellites cannot easily detect this micro-scale creep:

  • Landsat (30m): 1 pixel covers 900 m². A 50 cm border creep represents barely 5% of one pixel, lost in natural spectral noise.
  • Sentinel-2 (10m): 1 pixel covers 100 m². Mixed pixels fail to separate shaded coffee canopies from native tree crowns.
  • JRC GFC2020: The EU's 10m reference map is non-binding and often misclassifies shaded agroforestry as natural forest.

Institutional Bottleneck

The 'Red Book' Dilemma & Conflicting Forest Definitions

The second massive hurdle in Vietnam is proof of legality. Under EUDR Article 3, goods must comply with local land legislation. In Vietnam, land use is formalized through the Land Use Rights Certificate (LURC or Sổ Đỏ / 'Red Book'). However, the realities of the Central Highlands reveal a severe documentation gap:

1. Historical Agroforestry Expansion

During the 1990s coffee boom, hundreds of thousands of migrants settled on land managed by State Forest Enterprises. While these farms have produced coffee for over 25 years without recent clearing, provincial cadastre maps often still designate them as "production forestry land".

2. Decree 156 vs. EU Forest Definition

Vietnam's Forestry Law (Decree 156/2018) classifies forests by legal administration (Special-use, Protection, and Production forest). In contrast, EUDR adopts the FAO biophysical definition (≥0.5 ha, ≥10% canopy cover, trees >5m). A sustainable agroforestry system can be legally agricultural in Vietnam yet trigger an EU forest flag.

3. The 15–28% Undocumented Margin

Between 15% and 20% of Central Highlands coffee land (and up to 28.4% of surveyed smallholder plots in recent empirical studies) lacks official Red Books due to inheritance, customary transfers, or bureaucratic backlogs.

Solutions & Institutional Innovation

Adaptive Pathways: Moving from Surveillance to Relational Governance

To prevent the mass exclusion of smallholders, Vietnam is pioneering a series of public-private partnerships (PPP) and landscape-level interventions that bridge European standards with rural realities:

Landscape Approach

PPI Compacts (Production, Protection, Inclusion)

Spearheaded by IDH, MARD, and local People's Committees in Dak Lak (Krong Nang) and Lam Dong (Di Linh). Instead of auditing farmers one-by-one, entire landscape jurisdictions are mapped and verified collectively. By certifying whole communes as deforestation-free safe sourcing areas, individual smallholder verification costs drop by over 70%, while diversified agroforestry lifts farmer incomes by 30%.

National Infrastructure

National Digital Repositories (FORMIS & Terra-i+)

Vietnam's Department of Forestry is integrating its Forestry Management Information System (FORMIS) with agricultural cadastral maps. By synchronizing high-resolution satellite alerts from the Terra-i+ system with official Planting Area Codes, exporters can automatically cross-reference farmer coordinates against official 2020 forest baselines without predatory third-party subscription fees.

Value Chain Restructuring

Shifting from Market to Relational Governance

As argued by Global Value Chain scholars (Gereffi, 2018; Duong et al., 2026), European roasters must abandon arm's-length "spot-market" purchasing where risk is dumped backward. Sustainable compliance requires relational governance: multi-year off-take agreements, direct cooperative contracts, and guaranteed price premiums that fund continuous farmer training and data collection.

Roadmap for Inclusive Traceability

Four Non-Negotiables for a Just Transition

If the European Green Deal is to achieve genuine global solidarity rather than green protectionism, traceability systems must be rebuilt around rural justice:

1. Universal Cadastral Synchronization

Accelerate the national conversion of historical forestry land into official agricultural Red Books for smallholders with established cultivation prior to Dec 31, 2020.

2. Mandatory Buyer Cost-Sharing

Prohibit predatory contract clauses that push 100% of audit liability onto local exporters. European roasters should contribute to a national compliance fund ($0.02/kg).

3. Cooperative Data Sovereignty Trusts

Farm geolocation data must remain under the stewardship of local cooperatives. Agro-industrial platforms should access verified hash keys, not extract raw coordinates.

4. Preferential Green Credit

Establish collateral-free micro-credit lines to help smallholders finance sustainable shade-tree intercropping, soil regeneration, and digital cadastral registration.
